This post is about how I give spoken instructions to AI. The point is not precise wording. It is a few fixed moves: say why, draw a line around what it may and may not touch, let it ask first, and tell it to write things down when you are done.

Below I cover the four snags I started with, what I settled on, the numbers from this set of records, 8 lines I have actually said, and a starter prompt you can paste to an AI to run. If you want to know how I pulled these quotes out of transcripts and checked them, jump to the [appendix: how I pulled this together](#appendix-how-i-pulled-this-together).

## What was bothering me

The first snag was instructions with no purpose. A student asked an AI to list what could be cleared from a computer, with no target amount in the request. I had them redo it by stating the purpose first, then asking for the list:

> I think my goal is, to clear 10, at least 15 GB of space, so list for me what can be cleared safely.

> (translated from Chinese)

The second snag was an AI that keeps stopping to ask, so a person has to keep watching. I described my own change in class:

> I used to watch it and say, if something comes up midway, come back and ask me. Now I don't need to.

> (translated from Chinese)

The third snag was students who do not dare say they don't understand. Most are too embarrassed to admit they cannot follow an AI's output. In class I once said out loud, "I don't understand this, is it mahjong?", and only after that did students start speaking up.

The fourth snag was the same mistake coming back. Something I corrected once showed up again in a new conversation, because nobody wrote it down. What I do now is keep a list of rules the AI must always follow, which it reads before every job. But writing every complaint into that list makes the rules pile up and get messy. My line in class was that no country amends its laws every day.

## What I wanted, and how it changed

I want to treat an AI as a very capable colleague who does not know what is in my head. Say why, set the range, let it ask first, then let go. If I don't understand, say so. If I see a mistake, tell it to find the cause and write it down.

My own instructions are almost all long sentences typed by voice: spoken, full of filler, sometimes corrected halfway. I said this in class:

> I usually just talk, and if I say something wrong, I still keep talking, because it can tell which parts I got wrong and which parts I corrected later.

> (translated from Chinese)

So what works is not the wording. It is the 13 fixed moves in the four stages below.

[![Four stages, 13 fixed moves: opening with purpose and a boundary, midway examples and clues, checking with a separate reviewer, and closing by writing corrections back into a rule](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/1-four-stages-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/1-four-stages-en.jpg)

The turning points of the last few months, limited to what helps a reader:

**9/18.** Three things happened the same day. First, an AI was explaining a revision plan to me and I could not follow, so I asked for an example ("change two" and "dual track" in the quote are item names from that plan): "I still don't get change two, the dual track. Give me an example with one or two imaginary test takers and 5 questions so I can understand." I later turned that into a line I can call up any time, telling it to explain again in plain language a high schooler could follow. Second, when a discussion dragged on, I had it write what we had settled into a document as we went: "Our conversation needs to converge more and more, so you need to keep a document. The parts I agree with and have no doubts about, write to the file." Third, for checking the work I started bringing in a second AI whose only job is to find faults, without telling it where I think the problems are.

**10/1.** Before touching a website that is already live and in use, I set the priorities first: "Please remember, first priority is the site must not go down, second priority is don't lose data."

**10/4.** Before closing a session I now always ask: "What did I correct you on this time?"

**10/6 to 10/21.** I started doubting the rules themselves. On 10/6 I asked, "Will this make the rules more bloated?" On 10/19 I asked that each rule be written in only one place: "write it in one place only." On 10/21 I added a test: anything the AI already knows does not belong in the rules ("the model" in the quote just means the AI): "If the model already knows it, don't include it (the test is: would it know without this)." The same day I put my line on letting go into one sentence: messages to other people and promises made to them are the last things I hand to the AI. "Outward messages are the last thing I let go of. Everything else can be let go first, and I'll fix it if something goes wrong."

## What these records show

First the scope and baseline. There are two sets of material: transcripts of 30 one-on-one and company classes from April to October 2026, and 751 commands I typed to AI myself in my own work from June to October. For the transcripts, I had an AI pull out the commands I demonstrated in class, about 1,350 items. Then a script checked each original quote against the transcript, and 13 did not match. All 13 were dropped. I did not run a before-and-after comparison, so what follows is what came out of the sorting.

**Slips of the tongue do not hurt.** My commands in the transcripts are full of filler and slips, and they still worked. The mistake people new to AI make most is thinking a command must be precise. What to practice is whether the 13 moves above show up.

**Commands in class are short, my own are long.** In class it is mostly one thing at a time, because students must follow it and copy it. In my own work one message often hands off four or five jobs, separated by slashes or numbers, and the range I authorize is much larger.

[![In class versus on my own: command length, number of jobs at once, scope of authority, when to ask, and attitude toward rules](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/3-class-vs-own-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/3-class-vs-own-en.jpg)

**In class I mostly ask first, on my own I list decisions at the end.** Students are still building trust, so in class I mostly tell the AI to ask me first. I have settings that automatically block dangerous steps, so I dare to hold decisions until the end. A student's scope of authority should grow with their own safeguards. Don't copy how freely I let go today.

**Later, one more move appears: questioning the rules themselves.** In class records, a correction usually becomes a rule. In my own commands, later on, I more often ask whether there are too many rules. That question only shows up after some time of use, so it can wait until a student is in their second or third month.

## Commands I have actually used

These are 8 lines I actually said. After each, one sentence on how to swap in your own situation.

**1. Say your concern, your goal and your context**

> Okay, you tell it what your concern is, what your goal is, and what your context is.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: before opening, add three sentences: what I want to do, why, and what I will use it for. Leave the steps to the AI to propose.

**2. Let go, but draw the boundary first**

> Please check my computer for places where I can safely free 15 GB, then tell me, don't act yet.

> Just give me the list, right, just the list.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: after the authorization, always attach a boundary, such as "list it for me first" or "keep one copy of the original." If it can be undone, let it run. If it cannot be undone (deleting files, outward messages, live systems), keep it in your own hands.

[![Before handing over, ask whether it can be undone: undoable things are let go, irreversible ones are kept or listed first](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/2-boundary-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/2-boundary-en.jpg)

**3. Cannot say it clearly? Ask it to interview you**

> I have this idea, but it's hard for me to describe, that's fine, please ask me questions,

> If you are not sure, please ask me, don't decide on your own.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: when the need is not formed yet, say "I can't say it clearly yet, please interview me." For anything about your own internal details, add: ask if unsure.

**4. After it starts, no stopping**

> Anything I need to decide, tell me all together at the end, no need to stop in the middle.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: put it on the last line. Ask all questions before starting, and after starting, hold the decisions for one list at the end.

**5. Don't understand? Say so**

> I don't understand, write it in plain language.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: not understanding is the AI's problem, not yours. You can ask for one concrete example too.

**6. Odd result: find the cause before changing**

The AI kept making the same mistake. I suspected one of two things: the conversation had run so long that it forgot what was said earlier, or it never read the rules I wrote for it.

> Let's not change anything first, let's find the cause first. Is it that the context got too long and polluted, so the model forgot, or is it that the model never read those rules?

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: when an AI keeps getting it wrong, say "don't change anything," tell it to go back to the original data, report the cause, then discuss the fix.

**7. Keep the doer and the checker separate**

The sub agent in this line is a second AI I call up only to check the work.

> I send an independent sub agent to judge it, and what it is not sure about it tells me, and I check that myself.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: when it is done, have another AI review it, without telling it where you think the problem is. Whatever it cannot settle, it lists for you to check yourself.

**8. At the end, tell it to write it down**

> Why did you make the mistake? Right, and how do you think you can avoid it in future? Then, write it down.

> (translated from Chinese)

Swap in your situation: before you finish, ask once what I corrected this time. Keep only what it would get wrong again, and write it where it will read before the next job (such as the tool's custom instructions, or a rules note), each rule in one place only.

## Starter prompt

Paste this to an AI that can read your conversation records. Replace the brackets with your situation. The AI does the rest, and you only look at the result and decide which lines to adopt.

```text
Please check how I usually give instructions to AI.

1. Read my conversation records from (last week / last month), looking only at instructions I typed myself. (Records are at: __)
2. Mark each instruction for whether these four moves appear: stating purpose and use; drawing a boundary when authorizing (don't act yet, keep the original); letting you ask first or asking you to interview me; asking you at the end to write down my corrections.
3. Make a table: how often each move appears, with one quote of mine for each. For moves that should have appeared but did not, point to which instruction and what was missing.
4. The next thing I want to do is (__). Using the moves that were missing, write one opening instruction draft in my usual voice. Spoken is fine, do not make it precise.
5. Show me the draft, and I will decide which lines to adopt.
```

## Appendix: how I pulled this together

This section covers how I got from two sets of records to the lines above, for anyone who wants to do the same with their own class records.

### Two sets of material

The first is 30 transcripts from April to October 2026, covering one-on-one classes and company classes. The second is my own conversation records in Claude Code (an AI tool that reads and writes files directly on a computer) from June to October 2026, filtered down to 751 messages I typed myself and long enough to matter. Commands an AI sent to sub-agents and auto-suggested text were excluded.

### Extraction, checking, dropping

[![The pipeline: an AI extracts about 1,350 commands from 30 transcripts, a script checks each against the original and drops 13 that do not match; the 751 own commands are a separate line; quotes are then picked by topic and checked again](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/4-extraction-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/teacher-oral-instructions-to-ai/4-extraction-en.jpg)

For each transcript, I had an AI pull out the commands I demonstrated in class, each with a timestamp, the situation and the verbatim text. An AI's "verbatim" cannot be trusted as is, so I used a script to check it against the transcript: convert simplified to traditional characters, strip punctuation, then match by substring and sliding window. Of about 1,350 items, 13 did not match. All 13 were dropped.

### Sorting into fixed moves

I sorted by topic myself, using a keyword index and picking quotes one topic at a time. After the quotes went into the notes, I ran the same check again. For this post, I checked each quote against the notes once more.

### Transcript slips

Transcripts come from speech-to-text, so they have errors. When the notes hit an obvious one, I mark the intended meaning beside the quote.

That is all.
