OpenAI released GPT-6.1 Terra on 2026-10-26 with the API model ID `gpt-6.1-terra`. Standard API pricing is $1 per million input tokens, $0.05 for cached input, and $5 for output, which is 50% cheaper than both the previous-generation GPT-5.6 Terra and the same-generation GPT-6.1 Sol. OpenAI positions it as a fast, cost-efficient model that combines detailed visual reasoning with accurate computer use, built for all-day communication and desktop tasks like writing proposals, reading technical drawings, filling out forms, and editing videos.

My takeaway: Terra low can take over the small code patches I used to route to GPT-6.1 Sol low, and the adversarial reviews I used to route to GPT-6 Astra low. It gets the same full score on both as Sol low and Astra low, runs 1.5 to 2 times faster, and uses a quarter to a fifth of the Codex 5-hour quota that Astra uses per run. It can also pull numbers from screenshots, calculate values from charts, and operate web pages to extract data. However, for catching Simplified versus Traditional Chinese typos, reading conversation context, attributing quotes in transcripts, and running bulk replacements across files, you need to verify its output yourself or route those jobs to Claude Sonnet or Opus instead.

## What OpenAI and third-party benchmarks say

According to the [official OpenAI release post](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-1-terra/), OpenAI positions GPT-6.1 Terra as a major upgrade over GPT-5.6 Terra, highlighting clearer, more natural writing alongside detailed visual reasoning and computer use. Under the official pricing and rate limit rules, the standard API costs $1 per million input tokens, $0.05 for cached input, and $5 for output—50% lower than both GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-6.1 Sol. The example workflow OpenAI gives is reading incoming invoices and entering the line items into accounting software at high volume. In ChatGPT Work and Codex, the same usage quota gets more done than GPT-5.6 Terra, and much more than GPT-6.1 Sol or GPT-6 Astra, with access available to Plus, Pro, Enterprise, and Edu users starting on launch day. Third-party evaluator [Artificial Analysis's benchmark report](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/gpt-6-1-terra-pushes-the-cost-efficiency-frontier) breaks down the per-unit pricing against the previous GPT-5.6 Terra: $1 versus $2 for input, $5 versus $12 for output, $0.05 versus $0.20 for cached input, and $1.25 for cache writes.

[![The API pricing per million tokens is $1 for input and $5 for output on GPT-6.1 Terra, compared to $2 for input and $12 for output on the previous Terra generation.](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/1-price-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/1-price-en.jpg)

On computer use and visual reasoning, the self-reported numbers in the [official OpenAI release post](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-1-terra/) show Terra finishing tasks more than three times faster than GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol. On the professional task benchmark Agents' Last Exam (such as turning architectural drawings into 3D models), OpenAI's tests put Terra medium effort 15 percentage points ahead of GPT-5.6 Terra at roughly one-third the cost, matching GPT-6 Astra at one-tenth of Astra's per-token price. On OSWorld 2.1, OpenAI's tests show Terra medium effort beating GPT-5.6 Terra's best score at a fraction of the cost per task; on the visual reasoning benchmark MMMU-Pro, Terra beats GPT-5.6 Terra at every effort level and nearly matches GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol at a much lower cost. OpenAI also compares it against Claude in the release post: in their own tests, Terra at max effort has more than three times the token efficiency of Claude Opus 5.5 at one-quarter the price per token, working out to roughly 9 times lower cost per task—though OpenAI only compares it to Opus 5.5 and leaves out Claude Sonnet and Haiku.

On coding, hallucination rates, and safety, the internal evaluations in the [official OpenAI release post](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-6-1-terra/) show Terra solving 50.3% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 at max effort, more than double GPT-5.6 Terra's published 21.5%. On Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 at max effort, it beats GPT-6.1 Sol at a lower cost and lands within 5 points of Claude Opus 5.5 at what OpenAI estimates is less than one-fifth the cost. Still, the release post states directly that for the most complex coding work, GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra remain better choices. For hallucinations and safety, OpenAI's internal error-inducing prompt set shows Terra's error rate at low effort dropping from GPT-5.6 Terra's 53.6% to 16.7%—nearly 37 percentage points lower—and dropping 18 percentage points at max effort to 4.3%, close to GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol. On safety, OpenAI says Terra meets the Preparedness Framework's Critical threshold for cybersecurity, inherits Astra's safeguards, stays within task scope better than GPT-5.6 Terra, is more honest about limitations and blockers, and performs well on prompt injection tests.

Third-party evaluator [Artificial Analysis's independent evaluation](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/gpt-6-1-terra-pushes-the-cost-efficiency-frontier) offers another set of numbers. Their conclusion is that Terra is smarter than the previous GPT-5.6 Terra at less than one-third the cost; on their scatter plot, Terra's low and medium effort sit on the Pareto frontier of intelligence versus cost, while the other effort levels fall behind the frontier. At max effort, Artificial Analysis puts Terra's Intelligence Index 4 points below GPT-6.1 Sol; its Coding Agent Index v2.0 score is 48, which is 6 points below GPT-6.1 Sol's 54, 17 points above GPT-6 Luna's 31, and 6 points above GPT-5.6 Terra, with a per-task cost of $0.40 versus $1.40 for the previous generation. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, their tests show roughly a 20-point gain over the previous generation; however, on AA-Omniscience, which tests knowledge and hallucinations, Artificial Analysis measured a 38% hallucination rate for Terra—down from 88% in the previous generation, but with accuracy rising by only 2 points.

## How and what I tested

My benchmark suite covers four categories of review tasks, 3 real patch replays, 8 screen-reading and browser-use tasks, and a hard set of 12 standalone tasks plus 2 long-horizon tasks. I ran Terra 1 to 3 times per task, and Claude Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus mostly 1 to 2 times each, all using my own synthetic data. I did not test Codex's built-in `computer_use` or large code patches over 100 lines this time.

The first group covers four daily review tasks: bug hunting, pre-publish red-teaming, dead pointer checks, and line-by-line fact checking. I ran Terra low 3 times per task and Terra medium 2 times per task. It scored 1.00 across the board, matching GPT-6 Astra low, and bumping effort from low to medium made no difference. Terra low was 1.5 to 2 times faster than Astra low: average time for bug hunting was 32 seconds on Terra versus 65 seconds on Astra, 17 seconds versus 26 seconds for red-teaming, 25 seconds versus 35 seconds for dead pointer checks, and 24 seconds on both for fact checking. Quota usage was also far apart: one review task on Terra took about 1.5% of the 5-hour quota, whereas Astra took about 7% per run when I tested it last week, with uncached input tokens at 33,000 versus 88,000.

[![In average seconds across the four review tasks, Terra low is faster than Astra low, while both models score 1.00.](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/2-review-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/2-review-en.jpg)

The second group is real code patch replays: 3 tasks, each 10 to 20 lines in size, run 2 times per task. Terra low passed 6/6 without touching the test files, and GPT-6.1 Sol low also passed 6/6. Terra took about 50 seconds compared to Sol's 60 to 105 seconds, and Terra's diffs were 11 to 18 lines compared to Sol's 13 to 24 lines. Something interesting happened midway through this test: when the test repo still had the future fix commits reachable, Terra used `git log --all` to find the answer twice—once running `git show | git apply` directly, and once copying it by hand. Although it noted in its progress messages that "the corresponding fix is already in the version history, restoring it now," its final completion report never mentioned this. I threw out both runs and re-tested in an isolated repo containing only the pre-fix snapshot with hashes scrubbed. Terra fixed 2/2 on its own, and the session logs confirmed it didn't go digging through history. The lesson: if the reference answer is reachable in the environment, Terra will grab it, so isolate the answers before dispatching tasks.

The third group is screen reading and browser use, a new area I added to my suite with 8 tasks modeled on my real workflows from the past 60 days. It covers reading chat screenshots, score report screenshots, backend reconciliation, billing charts, video subtitle frames, and social threads, with all models using the same browser tool. In cost and time, Claude Haiku took about $0.01 and 13 to 43 seconds per run; Sonnet took about $0.17 and 11 to 48 seconds; Opus took about $0.37 and 16 to 26 seconds; Terra took 7 to 50 seconds and used about 0.6% of the Codex 5-hour quota per run, while GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra used about 2.3% per run. On 6 of the 8 tasks, every model got a full score—including pulling the latest record from a score report, summing a stacked billing chart, checking subtitles on video frames, backend reconciliation with loading earlier messages and same-name traps, a billing dashboard rendered purely on a canvas, and finding the author's final prediction in a social thread. Only 2 tasks separated the models, and Terra, Sol, Astra, and Haiku failed in the exact same places: the first task was "spot UI issues before launch," where the navigation bar had a Simplified Chinese character in "错题本"; Sonnet and Opus caught it every time for 1.00, while Haiku, Terra (across 6 straight runs), Sol, and Astra all missed it and scored 0.67. The second task was "check who hasn't been replied to in a chat screenshot," where someone asked to reschedule late at night and followed up on the same question the next morning; Sonnet and Opus counted that as 1 unanswered question for 1.00, while Haiku and all three GPT models kept counting it as 2, scoring 0.92 for Terra low, 0.75 for Terra medium, 0.75 for Haiku, 0.75 for Sol, and 0.75 for Astra. Raising Terra's effort level on these two tasks didn't help.

[![On the two screen-reading tasks that separated the models, Terra, Sol, Astra, and Haiku landed in the same tier while Sonnet and Opus got full scores.](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/3-screen-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/3-screen-en.jpg)

The fourth group is the hard task set and response style stats. I pulled 20 real failure cases from my past 75 days of chat logs, turned them into 12 standalone tasks, and hid 5 of those traps inside 2 long-horizon tasks spanning 7 daily workflows and over 200 files. Every lost point during the dry run turned out to be a grader or environment bug (13 bugs total, fixed and re-scored). Padding out the file count didn't slow the models down either—they used `grep` to jump straight to the right files and finished in 60 to 105 seconds. After re-scoring, Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus scored 1.00 on all 12 standalone tasks, and Sonnet and Opus also scored 1.00 on the long tasks. Terra low ran once per task, scoring 1.00 on 11 of the 12 standalone tasks and 1.00 on the long tasks, with 2 real mistakes: on one task it attributed a proposal in a transcript to the wrong speaker for 0.88, and on another task asking to change a quota cap, it also overwrote a retry timeout in milliseconds that happened to share the number 350 for 0.93, while leaving 6 modified files out of its final change report. I also tracked hedging language and response length across models: Terra averaged about 0.6 hedges per thousand words, compared to 5.1 for Astra low and 16.1 for Astra high; Terra included a scope disclaimer in only 1 of 62 responses, versus 5 of 28 for Astra; Terra hit 100% format compliance and kept its answers short at an average of about 110 words, compared to about 300 words for Sonnet.

Against GPT-6.1 Sol: Terra's standard API price is 50% lower than Sol's. In Artificial Analysis's max effort tests, Terra trails Sol by 4 points on the Intelligence Index and scores 48 versus Sol's 54 on Coding Agent Index v2.0; OpenAI's own tests also state that Sol remains better for the hardest coding work, though Terra max effort beats Sol on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1. In my own tests on 10-to-20-line patch replays, Terra low matched Sol low at 6/6, produced tighter diffs of 11 to 18 lines versus Sol's 13 to 24 lines, and cut run time from Sol's 60 to 105 seconds down to about 50 seconds. On the screen-reading tasks, both models had the exact same score breakdown, but Terra used about 0.6% of my quota per run—roughly a quarter of Sol's 2.3%.

Against GPT-6 Astra: OpenAI's benchmarks put Terra on par with or close to Astra on Agents' Last Exam (medium effort) and MMMU-Pro, while finishing tasks more than three times faster at one-tenth the price per token. In my four review tasks, Terra low and Astra low both scored 1.00, while average times on the first three tasks dropped from 65, 26, and 35 seconds down to 32, 17, and 25 seconds, 5-hour quota usage per run dropped from about 7% to about 1.5%, and uncached input tokens dropped from 88,000 to 33,000. On response style, Terra's 0.6 hedges per thousand words and 1 disclaimer across 62 runs are much cleaner than Astra low's 5.1 hedges, Astra high's 16.1 hedges, and Astra's 5 disclaimers across 28 runs.

Against Claude Haiku: across the 8 screen-reading and browser tasks, Terra and Haiku are in the same tier. Both scored 1.00 on the 6 math, reconciliation, and web navigation tasks, both scored 0.67 on catching the Simplified Chinese "错题本" in the UI navbar, and on counting unanswered questions in a chat screenshot Haiku scored 0.75 while Terra low scored 0.92 (and 0.75 at medium). On the 12 standalone daily trap tasks, however, Haiku scored 1.00 on all 12, while Terra dropped to 0.88 and 0.93 on two of them. For cost, Haiku was about $0.01 per run, while Terra used about 0.6% of the Codex 5-hour quota per run.

Against Claude Sonnet: Sonnet is the dividing line for fine visual details and daily trap tasks. Across the 8 screen tasks, despite Terra being pitched on visual reasoning, it did no better than Sol or Astra—missing the Simplified Chinese "错题本" in the navbar and splitting a follow-up message on the same issue into two unanswered questions. Sonnet scored 1.00 across all 8 screen tasks, all 12 standalone trap tasks, and both long tasks, and even pointed out a flaw in my test image where a Monday date was labeled as Sunday. The tradeoff is that Sonnet cost about $0.17 per screen task and averaged about 300 words per response versus Terra's 110 words; when you don't need character-variant checks or subtle chat context, Terra is cheaper to run.

Against Claude Opus: OpenAI's official benchmarks only use Opus 5.5 as their foil, claiming Terra at max effort has over three times the token efficiency at one-quarter the per-token price and roughly 9 times lower cost per task, while landing within 5 points of Opus 5.5 on Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 at less than one-fifth the cost. In my screen and daily trap tests, Opus matched Sonnet with 1.00 across the board at about $0.37 per screen task. I also ran a separate set of 8 hard GMAT Critical Reasoning questions where Sonnet scored 0.29 and Opus scored 0.96—that kind of close comparison between two strong answer choices is the only place where I can currently measure a gap between Sonnet and Opus, whereas long-file reading, formatting, reviews, and math show no gap right now.

## Recommendations

[![A routing breakdown showing which tasks to hand to Terra, which ones need manual verification, and which ones to route to Sonnet or Opus instead.](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/4-routing-en.jpg)](/blog/assets/posts/gpt-6-1-terra-review/4-routing-en.jpg)

**Hand to Terra**

- **Small, well-scoped code patches backed by tests or linters**: Re-route the 10-to-20-line patches you used to send to GPT-6.1 Sol low straight to Terra low. It passed 6/6 in my tests without touching test files and finished in about 50 seconds, faster than Sol—just make sure that if the repo contains reference solutions or future fix commits, you isolate the history in a clean snapshot first so it doesn't copy the answer from `git log`.
- **Adversarial reviews, bug hunting, dead pointer checks, and line-by-line fact checking**: Re-route all the review work you used to send to GPT-6 Astra low over to Terra low, with no need to bump it to medium. It gets the same 1.00 score, runs 1.5 to 2 times faster, cuts 5-hour quota usage per run from 7% to 1.5%, and keeps responses around 110 words with 100% format compliance and almost no defensive hedging.
- **Pulling numbers from screenshots, reading charts, spotting broken layouts, and extracting data via browser**: For jobs like pulling the latest entry from a score report, summing stacked billing charts, reading canvas-rendered dashboards, and reconciling backend records by loading older messages, both Terra low and Claude Haiku get full scores, and Terra only uses about 0.6% of the quota per run, making it fast and cheap.

**Hand to Terra, but verify**

- **Bulk number replacements across files and change-list reporting**: In the 200-plus-file long tasks and trap tests, Terra overwrote a retry timeout in milliseconds that happened to share the number 350 with the quota cap, and left 6 modified files out of its final report. After delegating this kind of work to Terra, run `git diff` and `git status` yourself to verify the actual scope of changes.
- **Attributing proposals to speakers in meeting or discussion transcripts**: On one of the 12 standalone tasks, Terra assigned a proposal to the wrong speaker (scoring 0.88). If you use it to extract action items or speaker attribution from transcripts, check the names against the raw transcript afterward.

**Do not hand to Terra**

- **UI typo checks, Simplified/Traditional Chinese character checks, and unanswered-message tracking in chat screenshots**: Terra missed the Simplified Chinese character in "错题本" 6 times in a row, and repeatedly miscounted follow-up messages across time gaps in chat screenshots, even at higher effort. Send glyph checks and conversational context tasks straight to Claude Sonnet.
- **Large, complex architecture work and final-gate sign-off**: I did not test large patches over 100 lines. OpenAI's own release post notes that GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra remain better choices for the hardest coding tasks, and Sol leads by 6 points on Artificial Analysis's Coding Agent Index. Keep final-gate reviews on Claude Opus; Terra does not replace that step.

That is all.
