## Timelines and Taste Aren't the Real Issue

People ask me how long before AI takes over completely. From where I sit today, two years is overly optimistic, and even five years isn't a given. But the real point was never the timeline. People keep debating data and judgment, and I just don't think that's where the focus belongs.

Another answer that often comes up is taste. Honestly, I don't think taste will end up mattering all that much either.

Look at the AI micro-dramas flooding the market. The visuals and plots reek of generative AI, yet they pull in massive audiences anyway. You know it, I know it, everyone knows they are AI-generated, but most people simply don't care. They still sell. On top of that, the next generation is growing up alongside AI from day one; it's entirely plausible that their aesthetic standards will just adapt to whatever AI feeds them.

## What AI Can Never Do for Humans

I've spent a lot of time turning over this question: Will AI eventually replace humans entirely? Things I once thought were safe don't feel quite so certain anymore, given how agents and robotics have developed over the past few years. So I started thinking from first principles: Is there anything AI truly can never do for us?

Stripped down to the bare essentials, the answer is remarkably clear: taking responsibility.

Put simply: AI doesn't get sentenced in court, and it won't go to prison in your place. When things go sideways, a human always has to carry the blame.

After all, we've already seen too many people on Threads posting AI screenshots to argue points without fact-checking, students handing in AI-written homework without a second glance, professionals using AI at work without knowing how to QA the output, and people vibe coding their way through projects while completely ignoring security and privacy...

## The Asymmetric Risk Premium Borne by the Frontline

When it comes to governance, I never intended to take things that far. I wasn't looking to train people for dedicated AI governance roles. What I really wanted was to help frontline workers build the practical literacy and discipline needed to use AI responsibly.

Still, the asymmetric risk premium dumped on everyday frontline workers was something I hadn't fully factored in.

What I'm arguing for is actually much more modest: anyone asking AI for output—whether executive or junior staff—needs the ability to catch errors and verify the work before signing off on it.

Coming from a background of teaching the GMAT, years of logic training left me with a habit: whenever I collaborate with AI, I trace its summarization chains and tool chains to see exactly where a step went wrong. I wrote about what this looks like on the verification side in [The Alignment Gap Is Closing. Next Comes Taste and Verification](/blog/posts/en/ai-alignment-gap-taste-and-verification/). Knowing how to pull apart a toolchain and spot flaws, rather than blindly trusting the output, is what actually protects your baseline.

## Back to Practice: Building Self-Verification Before Sign-Off

Teaching people how to catch errors and own their work will never be a popular sell in this market. It is nowhere near as flashy or intoxicating as vibe coding.

My plan is to start by sharing practical workflows—moving at a small, measured, and responsible pace while keeping an eye on the response, then adjusting course as feedback comes in.
