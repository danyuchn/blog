When I'm faced with something I don't care about, I can't sit still for even thirty seconds — even playing a YouTube video at 2x speed, I don't have the patience to finish it. But the moment something genuinely hooks me, I dive in headfirst, slipping into a flow state that lasts over ten hours without eating or sleeping, and getting interrupted can make me furious.

A lot of people with ADHD share this exact trait.

## The Two-Week Flow Filter

Throughout my life, more than 95% of new interests have just been fleeting obsessions that couldn't survive two weeks.

Yet if something manages to get past those two weeks, and I find that I can still sustain that flow state, chances are I can keep doing it for over a decade — and do it better than anyone else.

Luckily, the first thing that worked out that way was studying and taking exams. That led to a perfect score on the BCT, a 73 on the GSAT, a 760 on the GMAT, and a path straight through Jianguo High School and National Taiwan University.

## When ADHD Meets AI Tools and Teaching

Outside the things that completely absorb me, my ADHD basically makes me useless. And right now, the two things I'm genuinely obsessed with are AI and teaching.

Using new tools has only amplified that tendency. My biggest headache since picking up Claude Code is realizing that ADHD symptoms that barely showed up during childhood have been completely unleashed by AI.

Yet it comes with a major upside: when I'm obsessed with something, going deep comes naturally. When I step into teaching, I hold real respect for the craft I'm immersed in, along with an uncompromising personal standard to excel at it. Only the positive feedback from doing outstanding work loops back to trigger my dopamine.

## A Rule of Thumb for Spotting the Trait

If you notice a similar extreme in yourself — struggling to focus on everyday things, but falling into an unbreakable flow when the right topic hits — and you want to check whether you have these traits, the place to start looking is childhood.

Clinically, ADHD is overwhelmingly congenital; it isn't something people develop out of nowhere later in life, and formal diagnosis requires symptoms to trace back to childhood. Rather than judging by how you function today, ask your family, childhood teachers, or old classmates. Looking at how your attention worked across different subjects when you were young usually gives a far more accurate answer.
